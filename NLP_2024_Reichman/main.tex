\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Idiomatic expressions --- those whose meaning cannot be derived
compositionally from their individual words, e.g., \emph{``spill the beans''} or
\emph{``par for the course''} --- are routinely treated in NLP as a harder case
for language models than direct, literal phrasing. The premise is intuitive:
idioms are culturally specific and non-compositional, so a model must have
learned an expression rather than derived it, and models do degrade on
expressions they have not seen \citepf{tayyar2021astitch}. It is, however, rarely tested
\emph{in isolation}: any two rewordings of a sentence already differ lexically and
syntactically, so an observed accuracy drop after ``idiomatizing'' it could simply
be an artifact of rewording in general.

\textbf{Central research question:} does phrasing a task input
idiomatically cause a measurable degradation in a language model's task
accuracy, \emph{over and above} the degradation that any comparable
paraphrase (a non-idiomatic rewording) already causes?

To answer this cleanly, we rewrite every original example $x$ into two controlled
variants that share the same rewriting budget but differ in exactly one property:
$x_{\text{paraphrase}}$, reworded \textbf{without} idioms, the \emph{control}
condition isolating the effect of rewording alone; and $x_{\text{idiomatic}}$,
reworded \textbf{with} one or more natural idiomatic expressions and the same
meaning. The label $y$ is given to the rewriter and re-checked afterwards,
failures being dropped rather than relabelled (\S\ref{sec:augmentation}), and
each variant is evaluated under an identical
prompt template, so only the input's surface form changes between runs.
The question matters practically wherever informal text reaches a classifier
rather than being authored for one --- review and social-media sentiment,
support-ticket triage, content moderation --- because there figurative phrasing
is a property of the incoming data rather than a choice the system can make;
SST-2's own snippets are already partly figurative before any rewriting
(\S\ref{sec:datasets}). If idiomaticity has an
isolable negative effect, that bears on prompt engineering, augmentation
strategy, and robustness evaluation; if apparent idiom failures are merely
generic paraphrase sensitivity, effort spent specifically on idiom handling is
misplaced relative to general robustness-to-rewording work.

\textbf{Contributions.} We contribute (i) the paraphrase-controlled design
described above, which isolates the idiom-specific effect from the generic cost
of rewording --- instantiating for a linguistic property the control-condition
logic concurrent work establishes in general \citepf{yang2026compared}, and
finding a residual that survives its baseline on inference, unlike that
demographic case;
(ii) a \emph{sole-cause} diagnostic
that, restricted to items a model already answers correctly, identifies which
single rewrite is responsible when exactly one of the two flips the answer;
(iii) dual significance testing, pairing a continuity-corrected McNemar test
with a non-parametric bootstrap under different assumptions, guarding against
the chi-square approximation on small discordant-pair counts; (iv) breadth --- 14
instruction-tuned decoder LLMs from 0.5B to 7B across 10 families, on three
datasets spanning sentiment, knowledge QA and inference, for 42 runs with a
pooled cross-model test per dataset; and (v) a reproducible four-stage pipeline
with LLM-judge validation of every augmented example and per-artifact provenance
(\S\ref{sec:methodology}).

\subsection{Related Work}

\textbf{Idiom processing in NLP.} Prior work has mostly focused on
\emph{detecting} or \emph{translating} idioms rather than measuring their effect
on downstream accuracy. \citetf{tayyar2021astitch} probe pretrained models on the
literal-versus-idiomatic distinction, finding they do reasonably well given one or
a few labelled examples per expression but degrade substantially zero-shot on
unseen expressions, and \citetf{chakrabarty2022flute} introduce FLUTE, a
9{,}000-instance figurative-language benchmark spanning idioms, similes,
metaphors, and sarcasm. The LLM era has largely inherited this framing:
\citetf{phelps2024sign} find prompted LLMs competitive but still short of
fine-tuned task-specific models on three idiomaticity datasets even at GPT-4
scale, and \citetf{mi2025dice} show with a controlled contrastive dataset that
LLMs frequently fail to resolve idiomaticity when doing so depends on context,
arguing that headline benchmark success may rest on reasoning shortcuts. All of
this measures idiom \emph{comprehension} --- establishing it as a genuine weak
point --- rather than what idiomatic phrasing costs on a task that is not itself
about idioms.

\textbf{Figurative language inside a task.} Closer to our question is work that
scores figurative material within a downstream task. \citetf{stowe2022impli} pair
idiomatic and metaphoric sentences with literal counterparts and find that
MNLI-finetuned RoBERTa reliably recognizes figurative--literal entailment yet
fails on matched non-entailing pairs. For metaphor,
\citetf{sanchezbayona2025metaphor} conclude from NLI and QA experiments that LLM
performance is shaped more by lexical overlap and sentence length than by
metaphorical content --- an independent finding consonant with ours. Our design
departs from these in introducing the figurative property by a controlled rewrite
of items whose original form is also scored, rather than annotating it where it
already occurs, which is what makes a matched control arm possible.

\textbf{Why a paraphrase control is necessary.} Models are sensitive to input
edits that leave the correct answer intact: \citetf{ribeiro2018semantically} flip
predictions by \emph{rewording} inputs under semantics-preserving rules (SEARs),
and for LLMs the effect is larger still --- \citetf{cao2024worst} report a
45.5-point gap between the best and worst semantically equivalent phrasings of
the same input. A bare original-versus-idiomatic contrast is therefore
uninterpretable on its own, because it measures idiomaticity confounded with
generic rewording sensitivity.

\textbf{Controlled evaluation design.} Both our rewrites are invariance tests in
the sense of CheckList \citepf{ribeiro2020checklist} --- label-preserving
perturbations under which the prediction ought not to change. They are
deliberately the mirror image of contrast sets \citepf{gardner2020contrast},
which perturb minimally in order to \emph{change} the gold label. What we add is
subtraction: reporting the gap between a treatment and a matched control
condition instead of the treatment alone, the move that control tasks make for
probing classifiers \citepf{hewitt2019control}. Control conditions have appeared
in figurative evaluation before --- MUNCH includes deliberately inapt paraphrases
to detect lexical-similarity shortcutting \citepf{tong2024munch} --- but as a
distractor in the answer space, not as a matched arm whose accuracy is
subtracted. Concurrent work argues the general case directly:
\citetf{yang2026compared} observe that any counterfactual edit bundles the factor
of interest with incidental surface variation, and report that on MedQA a 14.9\%
flip rate under a demographic edit is statistically indistinguishable from the
14.1\% induced by paraphrasing alone. We take that logic as settled and
instantiate it for a linguistic intervention; unlike the demographic case, a
residual effect does survive here on inference. Studies that inject a property
without such a baseline, such as the dialect rewrites of GLUE by
\citetf{ziems2022value}, accordingly bound its contribution from above rather
than estimating it. To our knowledge idiom-inserted rewrites of general-purpose
NLU benchmarks have not previously been scored against a matched,
semantics-preserving paraphrase arm over the same items, which is what licenses
attributing the residual to idiomaticity rather than to rewording.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

\subsection{Pipeline overview}

The project is implemented as four independently re-runnable stages
(Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}) that communicate only through versioned files on
disk, plus YAML configs that set each stage's run parameters; dataset-specific
loading and answer-parsing logic lives in per-dataset modules. Any stage
can therefore be re-executed alone --- evaluating a new model does not require
repeating cleaning or augmentation --- and every artifact records the config
hash and model/prompt version that produced it.

\textbf{Stage 1: Clean.} Each raw dataset is loaded from Hugging Face and
reduced to one clean CSV of rows suitable for rewriting: rows outside a
configured length band are dropped (very short inputs leave no room for an idiom;
very long ones are expensive to augment and dilute the measured effect), text is
normalized (Unicode NFC, whitespace collapsing), exact duplicates are removed,
and every retained row gets a stable identifier that aligns all its downstream
variants. The label $y$ and dataset-specific metadata (e.g., MMLU's answer
choices) carry through unchanged.

\subsection{Augmentation into paraphrase and idiomatic variants}
\label{sec:augmentation}

\textbf{Stage 2: Augment.} Each cleaned row is rewritten by an external
LLM (Google Gemini) into two variants using two
distinct, fixed prompt templates. One Gemini sub-model is pinned per dataset for
cost reasons (\texttt{gemini-3.6-flash} for SST-2 and MMLU,
\texttt{gemini-3.5-flash-lite} for MNLI), shared by both arms, so the
paraphrase control stays internally valid within a dataset; only magnitudes
\emph{across} datasets are not cleanly comparable.
Templates are selected per dataset so wording suits the input type (MNLI,
whose rewrite target is a premise judged against a fixed hypothesis, uses its
own NLI-specific pair):

the \emph{paraphrase prompt} asks for a rewording in different words that
explicitly avoids idiomatic expressions, and the \emph{idiomatic prompt} asks
for one that naturally incorporates one or more idioms or figures of speech
while preserving meaning, register, and length. Both are given the task label
$y$ in the prompt itself, so a rewrite cannot silently invert the correct
answer, e.g., flipping a positive sentiment snippet into a negative one. An
external, deliberately stronger LLM is used rather than one of the models under
evaluation, since the augmenter's own idiom/paraphrase judgment must be more
reliable than the systems being tested.

Every augmented row then passes a validator chain: \textbf{label
preservation}, an LLM-judge check that the rewrite has not altered the correct
label; and \textbf{idiom presence/absence}, a per-variant LLM-judge check
requiring a genuine idiom in the idiomatic variant and none in the paraphrase.
Rows that fail validation after a bounded number of retries are dropped from
that variant (recorded in a sidecar manifest for transparency), which is why
the paraphrase/idiomatic CSVs are typically somewhat smaller than the
original CSV. An on-disk response cache keyed by (prompt hash, augmenter model,
input id) makes reruns idempotent and avoids re-incurring API cost.

\subsection{Datasets}
\label{sec:datasets}


Three datasets are evaluated, each scored with the accuracy metric used in its
original publication. Stage 2 rewrites one field per dataset --- SST-2's
sentence, MMLU's question stem, MNLI's premise --- and everything else,
including MMLU's answer choices and MNLI's hypothesis, stays byte-identical
across the three variants.

\textbf{SST-2} \citepf{socher2013sst} is binary sentiment classification over
short movie-review snippets; \textbf{MMLU} \citepf{hendrycks2021mmlu} is
multiple-choice knowledge questions spanning 57 subjects; and \textbf{MNLI}
\citepf{williams2018mnli} is three-way inference (\emph{entailment} /
\emph{neutral} / \emph{contradiction}) over premise--hypothesis pairs from ten
written and spoken genres.

The evaluated split differs by dataset according to what each release makes
available with labels: MMLU uses its \texttt{validation} split and MNLI its two
validation splits (below), while SST-2, whose GLUE test labels are withheld,
draws from \texttt{train} and \texttt{validation} combined, capped at 2{,}000
rows.


The three cover qualitatively different task types under one protocol: short,
informal, already-somewhat-figurative text; longer, formal, fact-oriented
multiple-choice questions; and sentence-pair inference. MNLI is the most direct
test of the three, because in sentiment or knowledge QA an idiom perturbs the
surface of an input whose answer is otherwise independent of it, whereas an
entailment judgement turns on a literal reading of the premise itself. Its
three-way label space also sets chance at 33\% rather than 50\%, which
depresses the per-row rate of sole-cause events relative to a binary task.

\textbf{MNLI-specific protocol.} MNLI pairs roughly three hypotheses with every
premise; emitting all of them would repeat the same premise and violate the
independence assumption behind the paired test, so the loader keeps only the
first hypothesis per premise. Rows come from the two validation splits combined
(matched, 9{,}815 pairs; mismatched, 9{,}832), the test labels being withheld.
The mismatched half contributes the five genres absent from training; each row's
genre is recorded, but the analysis does not split on it.
After first-hypothesis selection and cleaning, 5{,}127 premises survive to form
the MNLI original file, of which 4{,}709 (91.8\%) additionally clear validation
in \emph{both} rewritten variants and so form the aligned triples the analysis
runs on.

\subsection{Evaluated models}

Rather than a single-model case study, we evaluate a broad panel of 14
open, instruction-tuned decoder LLMs spanning 0.5B to 7B
parameters across 10 distinct model families and pretraining lineages:
the Qwen2.5-Instruct family (0.5B / 1.5B / 7B), the Falcon3-Instruct family
(3B / 7B), the OLMo-2-Instruct family (1B / 7B), Gemma-2-2B-it,
Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3, Phi-4-mini-instruct (3.8B),
Granite-3.1-2B-Instruct, H2O-Danube3-4B-Chat, Yi-1.5-6B-Chat, and
SmolLM2-1.7B-Instruct. Tables and prose below drop the
\texttt{-Instruct}/\texttt{-it}/\texttt{-Chat} suffix.
All models are evaluated zero-shot with an identical
dataset-specific prompt template applied to all three variants of a row, so
only the input text differs between runs; a device-agnostic runner selects
CUDA, Apple-Silicon MPS, or CPU and adjusts precision accordingly.

\textbf{Stage 3: Evaluate.} For each (model, dataset) pair, the model is
run on all three variant CSVs with the identical prompt, and predictions are
joined by row id, yielding one matched (original, paraphrase, idiomatic)
triple of predictions per surviving row. Per-variant accuracy is computed
directly from these joined predictions, scoring as incorrect any generation the
dataset's parser cannot map to a label --- a choice \S\ref{sec:results} re-tests
on the subset where all three variants parsed.

\subsection{Statistical framework}
\label{sec:stats}

\textbf{Stage 4: Analyze.} Two families of statistic are computed. The first
is per-variant accuracy and the two deltas between variants, reported
throughout in percentage points (pp):
\begin{equation}
  \Delta_{\text{par}} = \text{acc}_{\text{par}} - \text{acc}_{\text{orig}},
  \qquad
  \Delta_{\text{idi}} = \text{acc}_{\text{idi}} - \text{acc}_{\text{par}} .
  \label{eq:deltas}
\end{equation}
$\Delta_{\text{par}}$ measures the cost of rewording and
$\Delta_{\text{idi}}$ the additional cost of idiomaticity on top of it; the
research question of \S\ref{sec:intro} is precisely whether
$\Delta_{\text{idi}} < 0$. Each is accompanied by a paired-bootstrap
confidence interval and a McNemar exact p-value over the corresponding
discordant pairs.

The second, and our primary diagnostic, is a \emph{sole-cause} test, which
attributes degradation to a single rewrite type rather than to ordinary noise.
It restricts attention to rows where all three variants were produced and the
model answered the \textbf{original} correctly --- so any subsequent error is
attributable to the rewrite, not to the item being hard for that model --- and
then counts $b$ = \emph{idiomatic-only-wrong} (the idiomatic rewrite alone
broke an otherwise-correct answer) and $c$ = \emph{paraphrase-only-wrong} (the
paraphrase rewrite alone broke it). Rows where both rewrites agree, breaking it
or not, carry no information about which is worse and are excluded, exactly as
in any paired test. The gap between $b$ and $c$ is then assessed two ways:

\textbf{McNemar's test} \citepf{mcnemar1947note}, the standard test for paired
nominal before/after comparisons, with the continuity correction of
\citetf{edwards1948note}:
\begin{equation}
  \chi^2 = \frac{(|b - c| - 1)^2}{b + c}, \qquad \mathrm{df} = 1,
\end{equation}
testing the null hypothesis that $b$ and $c$ are drawn from the same
underlying 50/50 rate (i.e., among discordant rows, it is a coin flip which
rewrite broke it).

\textbf{Non-parametric bootstrap check.} Because that chi-square approximation
can be unreliable when $b + c$ is small, every result is cross-checked with a
paired bootstrap \citepf{efron1993bootstrap}: the aligned
rows are resampled with replacement 10{,}000 times, $b - c$ is recomputed on
each resample, and a 95\% percentile interval plus a two-sided empirical
p-value are read off the result. A result counts as robustly significant only
when both checks agree, as they do in all 42 per-model runs.

The same sole-cause statistics are additionally pooled across all evaluated
models for a given dataset (summing $b$ and $c$, and resampling from the
concatenated pooled rows for the bootstrap), with the explicit caveat that
this pooling is not a rigorous mixed-effects test, since different models
are not strictly exchangeable independent draws, and is reported as
indicative rather than definitive.

\textbf{One manipulation, three directions} (Table~\ref{tab:summary}). The same
idiomatize-a-matched-paraphrase intervention produces a significant
\emph{penalty} on MNLI (10/14 models, pooled sole-cause $p<10^{-4}$), a
significant \emph{benefit} on SST-2 (5/14 positive, none negative, pooled
sole-cause significant in the opposite direction), and a marginal penalty on
MMLU that survives only in aggregate (1/14 individually, $p=0.0011$ pooled).
Idiomaticity is therefore not a general accuracy tax; its sign and size track
how directly the rewritten span carries the decision.

\textbf{MNLI: the configuration the design predicts should show the effect.}
Ten of 14 models show a significant penalty, from Falcon3-3B at $-2.25$~pp to
Qwen2.5-7B at $-1.17$~pp (Table~\ref{tab:permodel}), and the sole-cause test
agrees --- $b>c$ for 12 of 14, both tests concurring at $p<0.05$ for 9 of the
same 10 significant models. Only Mistral-7B-v0.3 splits: a significant
accuracy delta with a non-significant sole-cause gap ($b=225$, $c=191$). Nine
models agreeing on two tests with different evidence bases is the strongest
single result in this study, and it lands exactly where
\S\ref{sec:methodology} predicted it should: entailment turns on a literal
reading of the premise, so a figurative surface adds a resolution step that
can fail. Table~\ref{tab:disagree} works one such row through in full for a
significant model on each dataset.

\textbf{SST-2: the idiom recovers what the paraphrase cost.} Original accuracy
is high and tight (83.8--91.7\%); plain paraphrasing already costs 12 of 14
models, significantly for 4, and idiomatizing that same rewording recovers
most of the loss --- positive for 12 of 14, significant for 5, with
Mistral-7B-v0.3 swinging widest ($-2.91$~pp then $+4.13$~pp). This is partly
genuine: SST-2's idioms tend to carry sentiment rather than obscure it
(Table~\ref{tab:examples}). It is partly artifact, addressed below.

\textbf{MMLU: a small effect capped by what gets rewritten.} MMLU spreads
models much more widely (29.7--68.7\%, against a 25\% floor) while both deltas
stay small ($+0.13$/$-0.79$~pp); only H2O-Danube3-4B reaches individual
significance, but the sole-cause direction is consistent enough ($b>c$ for 9
of 14) that pooling turns it significant. This is likely structural: Stage 2
rewrites only the question stem, leaving the answer choices --- where an MMLU
item's actual knowledge requirement sits --- untouched, so an idiom can
obscure what is asked without touching what the model must know. Models near
the chance floor also generate too few sole-cause events to have power
regardless (OLMo-2-1B: $b=8$, $c=11$).

\textbf{The SST-2 reversal is partly a parsing artifact.} Unparseable
generations score as incorrect, so a variant provoking more format violations
is penalised for reasons unrelated to idiom comprehension. Failure rates are
low on average (1.0\%/0.2\%/0.3\% for SST-2/MMLU/MNLI) but uneven --- up to
6.7\% for Mistral-7B-v0.3 on SST-2 --- and there the \emph{paraphrase} arm
fails more often than the idiomatic arm for 11 of 14 models, inflating
$\Delta_{\text{idi}}$. Dropping every triple with any parse failure leaves
MMLU and MNLI untouched (all 10 significant MNLI models survive) but cuts
SST-2's mean from $+1.35$ to $+0.76$~pp and its significant count from 5 to 3;
Qwen2.5-7B is the clearest case, its apparent $+3.04$~pp advantage becoming
$-0.21$~pp once its 6.4\%-vs-2.1\% parse-failure gap is removed. The direction
survives regardless --- even filtered, no SST-2 model shows a significant
idiom \emph{penalty}, only a smaller advantage.

\textbf{No capability or scale trend.} $\Delta_{\text{idi}}$ correlates with
neither original accuracy ($-0.13$/$+0.28$/$-0.29$) nor parameter count
($+0.10$/$+0.24$/$-0.30$) at any significance ($p>0.3$ throughout). The
Qwen2.5 ladder makes the point concretely: MNLI's penalty is non-monotone
across its three sizes ($-0.40$, $-1.59$, $-1.17$~pp at 0.5B/1.5B/7B), and all
four 7B models in the panel sit inside the degraded group rather than above
it. Rewriting is not purely destructive, either --- pooled across models, 419
SST-2, 235 MMLU and 1{,}614 MNLI rows were wrong on the original but right on
both rewrites.


\section{Discussion}

\textbf{The control arm is what makes the data legible.} Score idiomatic
directly against original --- the design most idiom studies use --- and SST-2
looks like a null ($+0.07$~pp, 1/14 significant) while MNLI's cost is
overstated by roughly 53\% (the naive $-1.96$~pp bundles in $-0.68$~pp of
plain rewording). The matched arm turns that false null into two real,
cancelling effects on SST-2 and corrects MNLI to its true size; neither
correction is visible without it.

\textbf{Where the effect lives matters more than whether it exists.}
Idiomaticity costs accuracy exactly when the figurative surface sits on the
span the answer is computed from: MNLI rewrites the premise a literal reading
depends on ($-1.28$~pp); MMLU rewrites a stem while the knowledge sits in
untouched choices ($-0.79$~pp); SST-2's idioms carry the very property being
classified, so they help rather than hurt ($+1.35$~pp). This is a ranking, not
a magnitude comparison --- MNLI's augmenter and rewrite depth differ from the
other two --- but the ordering matches exactly what a load-bearing-span
account predicts, and it is a narrower, more falsifiable claim than ``idioms
degrade LLMs.'' It tempers without contradicting the comprehension weaknesses
\citetf{tayyar2021astitch} and \citetf{chakrabarty2022flute} report directly:
what we measure is the residual cost after a model applies whatever partial
idiom understanding it has, on a task it was never asked to reason about
idioms for.

\textbf{The magnitudes are real but modest, and scale is not a fix.} A 1--2~pp
MNLI drop is statistically robust but the same order as plain rewording's own
cost, not an order above it --- idiomaticity is one distinguishable
contributor to paraphrase sensitivity, not a separate failure mode. No
correlation with scale exceeds $|\rho|=0.30$ across a 14-fold parameter range,
but with only 14 models $|\rho| \gtrsim 0.53$ is the significance floor:
absence of evidence, not evidence of absence. The practical upshot is a
triage rule, not a scaling law --- invest in idiom robustness where
figurative language can land on the decision-bearing span, not where a bigger
checkpoint is assumed to absorb it.

\subsection{Threats to validity}
\label{sec:threats}

\textbf{The statistical treatment has three known weaknesses.} The parse-failure
filter is blunt --- it drops 2.2\% of SST-2 rows on average but 12.4\% for
Mistral-7B-v0.3 there, and 8.2\% for OLMo-2-1B on MNLI, and dropping
rows non-randomly can itself
bias a comparison --- so filtered and unfiltered readings bracket the truth. The
pooled tests also treat models' items as exchangeable when the same row recurs
once per model, so a row that is idiom-hard for many models is resampled as many
independent observations --- clustering that narrows pooled intervals and makes
pooled p-values optimistic. No mixed-effects model was run, so pooled figures ---
including MMLU's, which rests entirely on pooling --- are indicative, not
definitive. No multiple-comparisons correction is applied either: 14 tests at
$\alpha = 0.05$ expect 0.7 hits under a true null, so MMLU's single one carries
little weight ($P(X \geq 1) = 0.51$) while MNLI's 10 and SST-2's five positives
are clear of chance ($8 \times 10^{-11}$, $4 \times 10^{-4}$).

\textbf{Scope limits.} Every model was evaluated zero-shot, greedily, under a
single prompt template per dataset, so few-shot prompting, sampled decoding and
prompt-format variation are untested and could interact with idiomaticity; the
panel is also decoder-only.

\subsection{Future work}
\label{sec:futurework}

The most valuable next step would sharpen the MNLI finding
rather than broaden it: a direct check that rewrites preserve meaning, ruling
out semantic drift as an alternative explanation. A mixed-effects re-analysis would
then put the pooled claims on a proper footing, and extending to few-shot
prompting would establish whether in-context examples recover the loss, as
labelled examples do for the fine-tuned encoders of \citetf{tayyar2021astitch}.

Three further extensions would test \emph{why} the effect concentrates where it
does rather than only \emph{that} it does.

Idiom opacity is currently binary. Scoring each inserted idiom's compositional
transparency --- via the augmenter itself, or existing psycholinguistic norms
--- and correlating it against $\Delta_{\text{idi}}$ would test whether cost
scales with non-compositionality rather than mere idiom presence. This reuses
data already collected, with no new model runs required.

The same logic applies to frequency: idiom rarity, via corpus statistics or
target-model perplexity, would test the familiarity account of
\citetf{tayyar2021astitch} directly rather than by analogy. Like the opacity
check, this is a post-hoc correlation against the existing 42 runs. A
dose-response relationship here would strengthen the mechanistic story behind
the observed penalty.

A third extension would turn the load-bearing explanation above from a
cross-dataset correlation into a manipulated variable, by rewriting MNLI's
hypothesis (or MMLU's answer choices) in addition to the premise/stem so idiom
position varies within one dataset rather than across three. Unlike the first
two, this requires new augmented data and fresh evaluation runs.

\section{Summary}

We measured the accuracy cost of idiomatic phrasing for 14 instruction-tuned
decoder LLMs from 0.5B to 7B parameters on SST-2, MMLU and MNLI, pairing every
idiomatic rewrite with a matched non-idiomatic paraphrase of the same row so
that the cost of rewording is subtracted rather than absorbed into the effect.
That control is what makes the result readable. Without it, SST-2 looks like a
null and MNLI's idiom-specific penalty is overstated by roughly 53\%. With it,
the penalty is confined to the configuration where the rewritten span is the one
the label is computed from: $-1.28$~pp on MNLI, significant for 10 of the 14
models, against $-0.79$~pp on MMLU with a single significant model and no
penalty at all on binary sentiment. Idiomaticity is therefore a distinguishable
contributor to paraphrase sensitivity rather than a separate failure mode, and
no scale trend is detectable across a 14-fold parameter range, though 14 models
could not detect a moderate one.
